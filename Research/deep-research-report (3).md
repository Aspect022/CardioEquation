# HR Conditioning Strength  
Existing ECG diffusion models typically **encode heart rate (HR) as a condition** but rarely add a large HR-specific loss weight. For example, DiffuSETS conditions on input HR and finds that including HR information *“significantly reduces the heart rate deviation”* between generated and target ECG【41†L559-L567】, without mentioning an extra HR-loss weight. Similarly, ECGTwin includes HR in its conditioning pipeline but does not report an explicit HR-loss term. In contrast, SE-Diff (physiology-aware diffusion) uses an auxiliary simulator loss with a small weight (λ=3×10<sup>−3</sup>)【33†L2430-L2433】. In other words, when auxiliary physiological constraints are added, their weights have typically been very low (≪0.1).  We found **no reference that uses HR-loss weights as high as 0.5 or 1.0**. Given your HR MAE remains large with weight=0.05, it is plausible that this is indeed too low; you may try increasing the HR-loss weight (e.g. to 0.5–1.0) and see if HR adherence improves. However, be cautious: overly large weight could distort waveform morphology. In summary, most papers do **not** prescribe a standard HR-loss weight, but they do show that *explicitly conditioning on HR* (rather than passive autoencoding) helps HR fidelity【41†L559-L567】.  

# Identity Preservation in Diffusion Models  
**Method:** State-of-the-art identity-preserving models integrate identity via conditioning modules, not by a separate identity loss term. For instance, InstantID uses a ControlNet-like “IdentityNet” conditioned on a face-recognition embedding and sparse facial landmarks. Its IdentityNet **“eliminates the text prompt and uses the face ID embedding as the sole condition”**【64†L139-L148】, forcing the model to focus on identity features. The training loss remains the standard diffusion denoising loss over both text and identity conditions (no special SNR-based reweighting)【64†L162-L170】. IP-Adapter and similar image-prompt methods likewise inject identity via cross-attention adapters, without an explicit identity-weighted loss. In summary, identity is preserved by adding strong identity input, not by a separate high-weight loss. 

- **Loss weighting:** These methods use the normal diffusion loss (constant weighting) for all conditions. InstantID’s loss is simply  
  $$L = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,C_{\text{text}},C_{\text{id}})\|^2\bigr]$$  
  where $C_{\text{id}}$ is the identity embedding【64†L162-L170】. There is *no mention of weighting the identity term by SNR or otherwise; identity condition is treated like any other input*. In fact, InstantID trains *without ever dropping the identity condition* (no classifier-free dropout on it)【66†L29-L33】, implying they keep a constant identity input during training. 

- **CFG channels:** None of these works use a separate “identity-only” CFG branch. Instead, they extend the conditional input (via adapters or ControlNets) so that identity is encoded in the conditioning. InstantID’s IdentityNet is effectively an identity-specific conditioning branch within the UNet【64†L139-L148】, but during generation it is merged with the text prompt (not a separate guidance channel). 

- **Guidance scale:** The recommended guidance scale varies by domain. Typical image diffusion uses high scales (e.g. 5–8) for strong conditioning. However, identity-preservation tasks often use **lower scales** to avoid distorting the person’s features. Audio/speech diffusion models routinely use scales around 1.5–3.0. For example, in AudioLDM the best performance on a captioned audio task was at *w*≈3【75†L61-L64】 (a text-to-audio model), and Tango (another text-audio model) also used *w*=3. We did not find explicit guidance-scale values for IP-Adapter or InstantID. Given your current *w*=3.0, it is moderate; you might try lowering it (e.g. 1.5–2.5) to see if identity fidelity improves without hurting diversity. (SE-Diff’s experiments also used CFG ~3 for physiological conditions【34†L1240-L1245】, suggesting that range is typical for signals.)  

# QRS Temporal Alignment Losses  
To penalize “compressed” QRS complexes, one can use time-warp or alignment losses. A leading approach from time-series forecasting is **DILATE** (Distortion Loss including shApe and TimE)【72†L25-L33】. DILATE explicitly splits the loss into a *shape* term (based on Dynamic Time Warping) and a *temporal* term (based on a time distortion index), with a tunable blend. It is differentiable and will penalize premature/late peaks. In other words, DILATE would give high loss to QRS that are correctly shaped but occur too early (narrow). For example, Leguen et al. (2019) describe DILATE to disentangle shape and timing errors【72†L25-L33】. Using a DILATE-like loss could address your 45% time compression by directly penalizing timing offsets. 

Aside from DILATE, other ideas include soft-DTW losses or CTC-inspired alignments. Standard DTW is non-differentiable, but smooth approximations (like soft-DTW) exist. Some speech tasks use CTC to align discrete events (e.g. phonemes) over time; one could analogously define a “QRS event” timing loss. However, we did not find any prior ECG diffusion work that explicitly adds a temporal-distortion loss. In summary, **use a time-aware loss** such as DILATE (with a shape+time term) to penalize mis-timed QRS【72†L25-L33】.  

# Guidance Scale for 1D Signal Generation  
Audio and physiological signals typically require **lower CFG scales** than images. Empirical results from recent models show guidance around 2–3. For example, AudioLDM’s experiments (on AudioCaps) found that *w*=3 gave the best Fréchet Distance (FD) and KL scores【75†L61-L64】. Similarly, models like TANGO and other text-audio diffusion use guidance ~3. By contrast, image models often use 5–7. We conclude that a guidance scale in the low- to mid-single-digits is common for audio. For ECG waveforms, a good starting point would be *w*≈2–3. A very high scale (like >5) might overconstrain the output. Given your current *w*=3.0, that is in line with audio best practices【75†L61-L64】. You may fine-tune in the 1.5–3 range. The “sweet spot” will depend on your data, but studies suggest using *w* around 2–3 for 1D signals.

# Differentiable HR Estimation  
Your current FFT-autocorrelation + soft-argmax is a sensible differentiable proxy for HR. We found **no standard alternative** widely used in diffusion contexts. In principle, one could train a small CNN to directly regress HR from the ECG (a learned HR predictor), but that becomes a black-box model and may not be reliable outside training data. Some related work does use deep nets on ECG: for instance, a deep ResU-Net can detect R-peaks with ~99.8% accuracy【90†L175-L184】, but that model outputs a segmentation, not a smooth HR value. DeepBeat (a PPG-based arrhythmia model) *“infers rhythm directly from the raw waveform”* without explicitly calculating HR【93†L25-L29】, showing an end-to-end approach, but this is for arrhythmia classification, not a regression loss. 

In short, **no known publication proposes a ready-made differentiable HR estimator to plug into diffusion training**. If desired, you could experiment with a custom 1D CNN or even a small Transformer trained to output HR, and backpropagate through it. Alternatively, you could incorporate R-peak detection into the network (e.g. using a differentiable peak-finding loss), but that is complex. Given the lack of clear alternatives, the FFT+softargmax approach is reasonable. You might consider smoothing the spectrum or using multiple autocorrelation peaks to improve robustness, but a fully learned HR model is more involved. 

# Flow Matching vs. DDPM (Conditioning Adherence)  
Flow Matching (especially Conditional Flow Matching – CFM/OT-CFM) is an emerging alternative to diffusion.  Recent works report that CFM can match diffusion quality with far fewer steps and potentially better control. For example, Tong *et al.* introduce OT-CFM, which yields **“simpler flows”** that are more stable and lead to faster inference【96†L59-L66】. They show that CFM improves performance on various conditional generation tasks【96†L65-L69】. Specifically for ECGs, Bondar *et al.* propose FlowECG (2025), a flow-matching model adapted from an ECG diffusion architecture. FlowECG achieves comparable or better metric scores than the diffusion baseline using *an order of magnitude fewer sampling steps*【98†L62-L70】. This demonstrates that flow matching can generate realistic ECG waveforms efficiently. 

Regarding your key question (conditioning adherence): none of the above papers explicitly test heart-rate or patient-ID conditioning under flow matching. However, the OT-CFM framework is theoretically designed to better approximate optimal transport maps, which could *help enforce conditional targets*. While we lack direct evidence, the fact that CFM handles conditional tasks well【96†L59-L69】 is encouraging. In practice, switching to a conditional flow-matching model could improve efficiency and might improve how strictly conditions are followed, but this is largely experimental territory. FlowECG suggests the approach works for ECGs【98†L62-L70】, so it would be worthwhile to try OT-CFM. In summary, **flow matching is promising** (faster sampling, good quality) and possibly beneficial for control, but direct gains in HR/ID adherence are not yet documented in the literature.
